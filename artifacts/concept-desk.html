The Concept Desk · a working demo

Speed was never the problem.

Lovable gives everyone the speed to build a demo. But the demo was never the slow part — the rework was: the missing users, the unverified data, the duplicate of a concept already in flight. The desk keeps the speed and catches the junk — through whichever door the idea arrives.

Door 1 · idea first

The same idea went to the gate twice.
Only one version survived.

This is the real Commentary/RAID concept from July, on the internal Connected Platform (“CP” in the quotes below) — a controlled experiment the pilot produced by accident: once uncoached, once through the desk.

1

The idea arrives broad Real

Four bets in one paragraph:

“PMs can't confidently manage risks, issues, decisions and commentary in CP — hidden behind a modal, free-text, hard to review. They fall back to offline trackers. We want to fix RAID capture, review, bulk updates, and reuse in status outputs.”
2

What the gate said, uncoached Real

The broad brief went to review without coaching. Verdict: Needs work — four bets bundled, situation too wide, outcome unfalsifiable. A full review round was spent learning what a coach surfaces in one question.

Criterion 2 · one bet — failed Criterion 3 · one user & moment — failed Criterion 4 · falsifiable outcome — failed Criterion 8 · scope stated — failed
3

The 30-minute conversation Reconstructed

The coaching replay. The questions are the coach's standard moves; every answer is condensed from the stakeholder's own words in the real briefs. Watch the last exchange: an “actually, I'm not sure” becomes a flagged open question — the same flag that became the verdict's one condition, and later the critique's #1 kill-risk.

CoachTell me about the idea the way you'd tell a colleague.
StakeholderPMs can't confidently manage risks, issues, decisions and commentary in CP — it's hidden behind a modal, free-text, hard to review. They fall back to offline trackers. We want to fix RAID capture, review, bulk updates, and reuse in status outputs.
CoachThat's four bets — capture, review, bulk, reuse. If only one could ship, which would teach you the most?
StakeholderReview. Status prep is where the pain bites hardest — that's where the offline trackers get created.
CoachPicture one PM. Where are they, what are they doing?
StakeholderThe day before a status meeting, opening one initiative, needing the current RAID position and what changed since last period.
CoachHow would you see that it worked?
StakeholderIn status prep, the PM finds every item changed since last period — and who changed it — in under a minute, without the modal or an offline tracker.
CoachAnd what are you deliberately not solving this round?
StakeholderNo new fields, no capture changes, no bulk view, no Sigma reuse, no data-model rebuild. Surface, filter, diff what CP already stores.
CoachWhat are you assuming the data already has?
StakeholderThat updates carry author and timestamp… actually, I'm not sure.
CoachThen that's a flag for the team, not an answer — it goes in the pack as an open question. That flag became the review's one “cannot verify” condition — and later the critique's #1 kill-risk. It was load-bearing.
4

The narrowed brief Real

One bet (review), one PM, one moment (the day before a status meeting), one countable outcome (every changed item, and who changed it, found in under a minute). Scope said out loud, assumptions flagged.

5

The verdict Real

Seven of eight criteria pass. The eighth — whether the who/when data exists — cannot yet be verified, so it travels with the verdict as an honest condition instead of being assumed.

Ready Criterion 7 · who/when metadata — cannot verify → routed to the Context Pack backlog
6

The prototype Real

The explore method built a genuinely interactive status-prep view from the Ready brief: surfaced RAID + commentary, type filters, a changed-since-last-review diff, and a flag-to-update tray — verified in both themes.

7

The receipt Real

A blind critique then pressure-tested the concept and surfaced three kill-risks: (1) the load-bearing who/when data is unverified; (2) “changed” is defined two ways; (3) the offline tracker may persist for reasons this bet doesn't touch. Its closing line — “Port” being the stage where a concept is rebuilt on the design system — was:

“Not ready for Port. Do not fund build until questions 1 and 2 come back green from a real PM session.”

That line is the junk-prevention receipt: the pack stopped an unvalidated build after producing a testable prototype — not instead of one. Speed kept; junk caught.

Door 2 · prototype first

The prototype already existed.
The desk asked it questions.

A stakeholder-built Lovable prototype — an initiatives tracker rendering 623 rows — arrived with no brief. Reverse-brief mode extracted the brief backwards, and recorded everything the prototype could not answer instead of guessing.

1

Before — the Lovable prototype Real

The original: a generic ShadCN/Tailwind initiatives table — 623 rows, search, filters, column and density controls, View/Edit row actions. Fast to make, and genuinely useful as a statement of intent. Also silent on everything the flags below record: no empty, loading, or error states, no permissions, no named data source. Its archived source is in the vault; the port in this act was made from it.

Image to follow The owner's original screenshot will be added here.
2

The interrogation Real

A UI encodes behaviour, not intent. The reverse-brief extracted what the prototype does build — scan, narrow, inspect, act on a large initiatives portfolio — and marked every extracted claim “confirm with owner”. What no UI can encode became the typed gap report: ten flags, each with a suggested owner. Nothing was invented to fill them.

3

The ten flags Real

open question What prompted this — which ticket, client ask, or number? The UI encodes no evidence. Prototype owner
open question What does success look like — what would be counted or observed? Prototype owner
open question What is deliberately out of scope? Nothing in the UI says. Prototype owner
open question Savings-variance column: where does the number come from, who maintains the calculation? Data team
open question System of record for the initiatives rows — which source feeds this table? Data team
risk Permissions (RBAC): the prototype shows every initiative and every value to everyone. Who must not see savings figures? Owner + platform
edge case Empty, loading, and error states: absent from the prototype — the port had to add them. Concept team
edge case Scale: behaviour at 623+ rows is undefined — the prototype assumes a table technology the design system doesn't yet provide. Build team
edge case Repeated row actions (“View”, “Edit”) lacked row-specific accessible names. Build team
duplication The initiatives surface is also targeted by an in-flight concept. Overlap unexamined. Concept desk
4

The handback — a brief and ten flags, not a build ticket Real

Door 2's output is understanding, not a build order. The extracted brief and its typed gap report go back to the owner: open questions carry named owners, edge cases are recorded, the duplication flag goes to the desk. From here the goal is a validated concept — a working prototype that proves business and user value, journey scenarios agreed in a matrix, and user flows drawn from it. None of that needs the design system yet, by design: speed of learning first.

Door 2's pack is mid-flight by design: the extracted brief and its gap report are on file in the registry, awaiting the owner's confirmation of the sections a prototype cannot answer — then the same verdict gate applies.

5

Later — when a validated concept graduates, the system lands it cleanly Real

Porting onto the design system is the build stage, after value is proven — never the door's goal. The receipt that it works when the time comes: this same prototype's source, rebuilt on Orbit components and tokens with the states the original never drew. Both platform themes, from one codebase:

18/18 · PASS scored blind against the written port benchmark rubric — nine dimensions, two points each: tokens, themes, full states, accessibility, density, contract match, pattern match, copy & motion, feel.

After the verdict

A cleared concept earns a second stage.

Concepts whose verdict is Ready graduate to a Definition Pack: a scenario & behaviour matrix agreed with the tool owner and the data team, rendered as interactive journey flows. The gap report seeds it — edge cases become edge scenarios, open questions become clarifications with owners. The matrix is authored in Excel — where the tool owner and data team already work — with the vault holding the canonical copy, and the user flows are generated from it. Together with the working prototype, this is the desk's end state: a validated concept. Only then does the build team port it onto the design system. This one is real, from a live product surface:

R4

The comment IS the negotiation guide

An agreed rule: the per-clause comment holds the current position — AI recommendation, user edit, or accepted supplier wording — and everything is evaluated against it.

G-01

Round 1 — first upload, supplier detected

A green-path scenario with its full stage rail: user actions, backend, front-end result, next action, guaranteed outcome — and a worked example with real-shaped numbers.

E-07

Regression on an accepted clause

An edge scenario that explains a design decision: why “accept” stores the wording instead of deleting the row — so a quiet change three rounds later gets caught.

C1–C6

Six open clarifications, each with an owner

The honest list of what is not yet agreed — recorded, owned, and blocking build rather than silently resolved.
